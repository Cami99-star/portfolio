01 The problem

Sharing a device also means
sharing time and context.

A meeting does not begin when someone presses Record. It begins when they need to know whether the device is available, how to coordinate its use, and where the meeting content will go afterward.

The project addressed uncertainty across these connected moments, rather than treating device connection as an isolated interaction.

Before the meeting

“Is the device available?”

Without a clear schedule, people have difficulty anticipating whether the shared device is booked or in use.

At connection

“Is someone still using it?”

Back-to-back use makes session boundaries and access to the device important to understand.

After the meeting

“Where is our meeting content?”

People need a clear path back to the relevant meeting information and its AI-generated summary.

Scenario questions illustrate the design problem; they are not verbatim participant quotes.

02 Why this project

An existing product.
A focused internship contribution.

M-echo combines an onsite meeting device with a companion app. Shuzhuo Technology was exploring integrated hardware and software experiences for in-person meetings.

During my one-month internship, I independently designed and revised the app flows presented in this case study, with guidance and review from my mentor. My work covered connection and device information, then extended to device reservation and meeting scheduling.

I owned the interface design and revisions shown here, working independently with mentor guidance. The wider product strategy, hardware development, and existing ancillary features were outside my design scope.
03 The opportunity

Make coordination visible
before connection.

I framed the design around three practical needs: understand the schedule, reserve the device for a meeting, and return to meeting information that can be shared.

Success meant people could complete these core workflows in the revised app. I evaluated those tasks after the design changes, rather than claiming a measured improvement over an earlier version.

Design question

How might we help people plan around a shared meeting device, with a clearer path from reservation to meeting content?

04 Design decisions

From a device connection
to a connected workflow.

I organized the revised experience around three moments: understanding the schedule, making a new reservation, and accessing meeting information.

Decision 01 / See the plan

Bring the calendar
into the meeting workflow.

The calendar and timeline bring planned meetings into view. Rather than asking people to infer availability from a connection state, this gives them schedule information to consult before starting a meeting.

Calendar + timeline

See a day in context.

A calendar provides the broader view; the timeline below makes that day’s meetings concrete.

  • Choose a date.
  • Review its scheduled meetings.
  • Open the relevant meeting information.
Decision 02 / Make a plan

Connect device reservation
with meeting scheduling.

The new flow supports both reserving the shared device and creating a meeting schedule. These are related needs: securing access to the hardware and organizing the meeting it will support.

Reservation + scheduling

A next step within
the app.

The revised experience brings creation into the daily planning flow, alongside the meeting and task information users are already reviewing.

  • Start from the day’s planning view.
  • Create a new meeting reservation.
  • Return to the meeting schedule.

The revised flow connects device reservation and meeting scheduling within the companion app.

Decision 03 / Keep context

Make meeting details
easier to return to and share.

The schedule view organizes meetings into a scannable list. Sharing meeting content is part of the wider workflow: the evaluation included locating and sharing an AI-generated meeting summary, not a verbatim transcript.

Meeting context

From the day’s plan
to the information that matters.

Meeting lists and supporting actions keep relevant information accessible within the app.

  • Review meeting details in the schedule.
  • Locate the relevant meeting content.
  • Share the AI-generated meeting summary.

Schedule view from the revised interface. Sharing the AI-generated summary was evaluated as a separate task.

This was one evaluation round with a small sample, without a baseline comparison. Task completion does not establish faster performance, zero errors, unassisted use, or fewer real-world booking conflicts. I have not confirmed whether the revisions were released, and post-release data was not available to me.

06 Reflection

The experience starts
before connection.

This project shifted my attention from individual screens to the shared workflow around a device. A connection flow alone does not explain when a device is available or how a meeting fits into someone’s day.

My contribution was a focused set of app designs and revisions, followed by an evaluation of the revised workflows. The next step would be to investigate how well these interactions hold up when people share a device across consecutive meetings.

What I would test next

Back-to-back reservations, overlapping requests, and how people recover when their plans change. These scenarios would help reveal friction beyond successful completion of the three core tasks.

What I would verify with engineering

How device reservations, active sessions, and generated meeting summaries are associated. Clear interface communication needs reliable system behavior behind it.
