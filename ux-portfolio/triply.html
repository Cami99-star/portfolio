01The problem

A trip starts with excitement.
Planning can scatter it.

The project begins with a planning scenario: a traveler has saved ideas, a budget, and a few dates, but still needs to turn them into a practical itinerary. Research, comparisons, and bookings can end up spread across different tools.

Triply explores how a single planning workspace could keep the conversation, itinerary, and map connected while leaving room to change the plan.

Collect ideas

Too many places to look.

Ideas and useful tips can become disconnected from the plan they are supposed to support.

Build the itinerary

Every choice affects another.

Where to stay, what to do, travel time, and cost need to make sense together.

Make it your own

A generated plan is only a start.

Travelers need to review suggestions and adapt the itinerary to their preferences.

02Why this project

Connect the planning journey,
not just the search results.

The project materials include a comparison of travel-planning services, a journey map, an interface system, and detailed itinerary screens. The journey extends from destination inspiration to preparation and the trip itself.

The proposed product brings an AI assistant, a structured itinerary, and a map into one workspace. This case study focuses on how the interface organizes those relationships, rather than presenting a live booking service.

I designed this solo project over six weeks, including the planning flow, detailed workspace, design system, and supporting review and sharing screens. The PDF documents a high-fidelity web app concept and a proposed business model, rather than a launched booking service.

03The opportunity

Turn scattered choices
into a plan people can shape.

The design opportunity is not simply to generate more recommendations. It is to help a traveler understand a proposed trip, compare options, and make changes without losing the overall picture.

The concept is organized around a short setup flow, a connected itinerary workspace, and a way to review and share the result.

Design question

How might we help travelers turn scattered ideas into a coherent itinerary they can understand, adjust, and share?

04Design decisions

Three moments.
One connected experience.

The following decisions connect the project’s user needs with its proposed interface and product behavior.

Decision 01 / Set the direction

Ask for context
before building the plan.

The setup screens gather destination, dates, and the type of trip. Splitting these decisions into focused steps gives the proposed itinerary a clearer starting point.

Proposed setup screens from the project. These are interface stills, not a working trip-generation demonstration.

Decision 02 / Keep the whole trip in view

Connect the assistant,
the itinerary, and the map.

The detailed planning screen puts the conversation beside a structured itinerary and a map. Suggestions can be read in the context of the day, rather than as a separate list of search results.

Keep the whole trip in view

One workspace.
Several connected decisions.

The proposed layout keeps planning context close to the details a traveler needs to review.

  • Review flights and places to stay.
  • See activities, dining, and shopping within the itinerary.
  • Use the map to relate suggestions to the day’s route.
Decision 03 / Review and share

Make the plan
easier to carry forward.

The checkout and sharing concepts extend the planning flow beyond recommendations. An expense summary brings the proposed trip together, while a dedicated sharing view presents it as a plan someone else can understand.

Checkout and sharing are design proposals. Live transactions, booking fulfilment, and provider integrations are not claimed.

05Outcome & next evaluation

A connected planning concept.
A clear next step to evaluate.

The project produces a proposed flow from planning inputs to a detailed itinerary, with supporting review and sharing screens. It demonstrates an approach to information organization and interaction design; the materials do not yet establish task-performance results.

06Reflection

A useful plan must
stay understandable when it changes.

The key design question is how an AI-assisted plan remains understandable and editable. A generated itinerary should help someone make decisions, not conceal how those decisions fit together.

What I would test next

Ask travelers to plan a trip, replace a recommendation, check the day’s route, and share the result. Observe where they lose context or need to leave the workspace.

What I would verify next

Verify recommendation sources, live prices, availability, and how changes propagate through the itinerary before treating the concept as a booking product.
